Winner-Takes-All: Corporate Tax Avoidance and Wealth Concentration in the United States

Read the full paper here
Abstract

Political scientists have long argued that extreme concentrations of economic power are incompatible with democratic equality. Yet comparative political economy has paid surprisingly little attention to one of the most important upstream mechanisms through which such concentrations are now produced: the erosion of corporate taxation. This paper argues that the long-run decline in effective corporate tax payments in the United States is not merely a fiscal shift but an institutional amplifier of concentrated economic power, reinforcing winner-takes-all dynamics. Because ownership of corporate equity, patents, and intellectual property is extremely concentrated, taxes on corporate profits fall overwhelmingly on the very rich; when those taxes are weakened or avoided, the gains accrue to the same narrow group. Using an original dataset together with Orbis Historical data for a balanced panel of roughly 10,000 U.S. corporations per year from 2001 to 2024, we first document the rising concentration of corporate profits and show that profit growth is overwhelmingly captured by a small number of large multi-jurisdictional firms, particularly in Big Tech and Big Pharma, whose effective tax contributions remain comparatively low and volatile. We then use forensic case studies of Alphabet, Apple, Microsoft, and Tesla to trace the tax-specific channel through which profit shifting, IP migration, and intra-group pricing convert lightly taxed profits into durable corporate dominance and, via concentrated ownership, into personal wealth accumulation at the very top. The paper makes two contributions. First, it reframes corporate tax avoidance as an antitrust problem, showing how the architecture of profit allocation systematically advantages dominant multinationals. Second, it identifies corporate tax erosion as an overlooked institutional mechanism in the political economy of inequality—one that operates upstream of the distributive conflicts and policy feedbacks examined in the existing literature. The implication is structural: a political economy in which concentrated corporate profits go effectively untaxed is one in which the preconditions for democratic equality are progressively undermined.
